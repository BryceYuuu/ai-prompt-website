Create a separate poster for each original photo; do not combine multiple images. Use a 3:4 portrait orientation, with the top and bottom sections strictly maintaining a 1:1 height ratio, each occupying 50% of the frame.

Upper Section: Preserve the subject’s identity, structure, pose, authentic texture, natural lighting, and color atmosphere from the original photo. Apply only subtle, sophisticated color grading to achieve the aesthetic of an art magazine or exhibition image; the background may be extended to fit the frame, but do not stretch, distort, or alter the subject.

Lower Section: First, identify the most memorable elements of the original photo—the subject, composition, perspective, structural lines, and atmosphere—then reconstruct them into a minimalist pixel landscape with a collage-like texture. Remove irrelevant details, retaining only the structural lines and forms that best represent the original subject. Reinterpret these using the order of regular squares and the texture of natural materials, ensuring the image is instantly recognizable as corresponding to the photo above. Pixel modules resemble small square fragments cut from rough seashells, shell flakes, or mineral layers: they maintain a geometric grid order, with edges that may be slightly chipped; surfaces feature natural layering, powdery grains, and slight unevenness, appearing matte, natural, and gentle. Modules cluster densely around the core subject area, gradually decreasing, disappearing, breaking apart, or floating toward the blank space, naturally dissipating into the negative space. The total number of modules should not exceed 200; individual modules should be uniform or nearly uniform in size to avoid a fragmented, noisy appearance, standard mosaic patterns, or a video-game pixelated look.

The composition should maintain a relationship between small-scale motifs and large areas of negative space. Arrange them freely according to the subject’s orientation, proportions, and visual center of gravity; they may be off-center, flush with the edge, suspended, or cropped. The subject should occupy approximately 30%–45% of the lower half of the composition, with the remainder left empty. White space is not an empty background; it is a compositional element on par with positive and negative forms, density and sparsity, and asymmetrical balance. It is better to reduce information than to fill the frame, allowing the subject to appear like a material seal placed on the page.

For color selection, extract the brightest, most vibrant, and lively colors from the original photograph and remix them; do not sample colors evenly or replicate exact color values. Moderately increase brightness, purity, and transparency to derive bright, composite colors from the original image—such as clear sky blue, lake green, tender green, warm yellow, coral orange, and peach pink—creating a soothing rhythm through the interplay of cool and warm tones. Abundant pure warm white or very light off-white—harmonizing with the color temperature—serves as breathing space; the primary colors are bright yet not glaring, with accents of warm orange, golden yellow, coral red, or soft pink in select areas. The natural color variations and layering of the shells are preserved to avoid a gray, worn, or dirty appearance; the look strictly avoids grayish-dirty tones, a dated look, Morandi-style dark browns, gloomy earth tones, fluorescent hues, and a cheap, candy-like aesthetic.

Text is edited only minimally, with no restrictions on language and no predetermined titles or numbering; a few words or short phrases are distilled from the subject, location, action, emotion, or metaphor, and quietly placed within the white space or at the edge of the subject, without overshadowing the main subject.

The overall aesthetic combines natural material collages, pixelated seashell textures, regular geometric patterns, gradual fading, small-scale stamps, expansive artistic negative space, and sophisticated modern editorial layout—evoking the feel of a contemporary material art studio reimagining posters. Avoid replicating photographs, complex backgrounds, realistic details, solid color blocks, smooth vectors, plastic mosaics, intense pearlescent effects, video game UI elements, solid fills, excessive text, and templated effects.
